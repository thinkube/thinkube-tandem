= Your first thing built
:page-type: tutorial

_From an empty box to a change running in your project, one screen at a time. The example is a todo app made from the web app template and nine sentences about it._

.TL;DR
Say to Claude Code: "Make an app called todo from the web app template, then put these lines in the box of a new thinking space:"

[source,text]
----
My tasks come back in a sensible order, what is due soonest first.
Finished tasks stop crowding the ones I still have to do.
Deleting a task asks me first.
----

Claude creates the app and calls `save_draft`. Then you open the space and press the one button the strip offers, five times: *Read these 3*, *Keep these 3*, *Group into things to build*, *Build the first*, *Build these N*. When the run ends you read the report and press *Go on to the next*. What you have afterwards is the change merged, deployed and running at the app's address.

== What you will learn

* How to tell Tandem what you want, and how to see whether it understood you.
* What each button does before you press it, and why it exists.
* How to read the work page, the run and the report.
* What the three decisions at the end mean.

== What you need

* Thinkube IDE with the Tandem icon in the activity bar.
* A repository Tandem knows: *Tandem: Activate Repository* on one you have, or "make an app called todo from the web app template" said to Claude Code, which creates one and activates it.

== 1. Open the space

Every thinking space is one page, and you only ever look at that page. Click the Tandem icon. Under *Projects*, your repository lists its thinking spaces; *New thinking space…* makes one. Each space opens in its own editor tab, titled with the space's name.

If your workspace has more than one folder, Tandem asks which repository you mean and never guesses. The status bar shows the choice as *Tandem: <product> / <repository>*.

== 2. The strip and the four pages

The strip is there so that you never have to work out what to do next. Across the top of the space is one line saying where you are, such as *9 lines written, not read*, and one button, the next thing you can do. Under the strip are the names of the four pages: *the box*, *your sentences*, *what it will do*, *the run*. The strip moves you forward; the page names let you look back, and *back to now* returns.

[d2,alt="The four pages of the space in the order the strip moves through them: the box, your sentences, what it will do, the run"]
....
direction: right
box: "the box\nwrite, read"
sentences: "your sentences\nkeep, group"
work: "what it will do\npromises, sign"
run: "the run\ncards, then the report"
box -> sentences -> work -> run
run -> sentences: "Go on to the next" {style.stroke-dash: 4}
....

== 3. Write in the box

The box is where your words go, and it records nothing on its own, so writing costs nothing. Type what you want, one line per thing, in the words a person would use. The line under the box says it as it is: *one line is one ask · Enter starts another · Ctrl+Enter reads · nothing is recorded by reading*.

The nine lines this page follows:

[source,text]
----
My tasks come back in a sensible order — what is due soonest first, and high
priority above low — instead of whatever order the database felt like.
A task that is past its due date is obvious at a glance, not grey small print.
Finished tasks stop crowding the ones I still have to do.
I can see how many tasks I have without counting cards.
I can look at just the high priority ones when I want to.
When I open the new-task box the cursor is already in the title, so I can just type.
I can add a task with the keyboard alone — type a title, press Enter, done.
If I try to save a task with no title it tells me, instead of doing nothing.
Deleting a task asks me first, because one mis-click should not lose work.
----

Claude Code can write the lines for you with Tandem's `save_draft`. It cannot press anything after that.

== 4. Read these 9

Reading shows you how the machine understood you before anything is recorded, so a misreading costs one press, not a build. The machine reads two things in each sentence and nothing else: what it is about, and what must become true of it. Press *Read these 9*. Every sentence comes back with those two parts marked: the subject highlighted, the claim underlined. Above them, *What I read in your words* lists the subjects it found, *my tasks*, *the new-task box*, *deleting a task*, with how many claims each carries. Sentences that name the same subject meet under it; that is how the nine lines become three things in step 6 without you sorting them. A sentence that never names its subject shows the subject in brackets and the word *names nothing*, so you can see that the machine guessed. xref:from-sentence-to-promise.adoc[How a sentence becomes a promise] follows one sentence all the way to its checks.

image::tandem-01-read.png[The space after Read these 9: each sentence marked with what it is about and what must become true of it, under three subjects, and Keep these 9 on the strip]

If the reading is wrong, change the words and press *Read it again*. Nothing was recorded.

== 5. Keep these 9

Keeping is the moment your words become the record, and the record is never edited by the machine. Press *Keep these 9*. The page *your sentences* opens with the nine sentences, each with a pencil to say it differently and a plus to amend it. Everything the machine derives from here can be thrown away and made again; these lines cannot.

image::tandem-02-keep.png[The kept sentences, each with a pencil, and Group into things to build on the strip]

== 6. Group into things to build

Small things build and get judged on their own, so the machine proposes how to cut your sentences into things worth delivering separately. Press *Group into things to build*. Each thing gets a name in your register, the sentences it carries, and a word for the suggested order: *first*, *then*, *later*. Proposing decides nothing.

image::tandem-03-group.png[The sentences became three things: first, then, later]

== 7. Build the first

Working a thing out is what costs, so it happens only for the thing you pick. Press *Build the first*. The strip says *working out what to build — N of M subjects done* and the banner on the page counts the same. When the last subject is done, the page *what it will do* opens by itself.

It shows, under *What will be true when this is done*, one promise for each claim the reading found, the files it lands in, and under each promise the lines that must become true. Each line says where it stands: *checked when it is built* means a check will prove it, and the check is written before the code by a worker that never sees the code; *judged by a reviewer at delivery* means a reviewer reads the finished work once; *only you can see this* means only the running product can show it, and you confirm it.

image::tandem-06-work-page.png[The what-it-will-do page: promises, the files they land in, and each line with where it stands]

On *your sentences*, under the thing in hand, *N things I decided for you* lists the choices your sentences left open, each with the answer the machine chose, for you to replace before you sign.

One promise is added by the machine to every thing: *The documentation says what "<the thing>" does, in the words of the person using it.* It lands a page in your project's `docs/` folder and a reviewer judges it at delivery, like the other lines. Press *Not needed* on it, with your reason, if no page applies; without the page or that press, the work cannot be signed. Documentation is not a step after the work; it is one of the promises the work keeps.

== 8. Sign

Signing freezes exactly what you saw, so what runs is what you approved and nothing else. At the bottom of the work page, *Pressing Build these N* says what the button does, in the page's words: it signs this work and locks the sentences behind it, records the machine's own answers to the open questions, starts the workers, and pushes a branch; nothing is merged until you accept the delivery.

image::tandem-07-sign.png[The work page of a thing with one sentence: two promises, their lines, and under Pressing Build these 2 what the button does]

Press *Build these N*. The strip changes to *building — <the thing's name>*, the run page opens on *preparing the tree*, and *Stop* is the only other button.

image::tandem-08-signed.png[The moment after signing: the strip says building, the run page opens on the door, and Stop is the only other button]

== 9. Watch it build

Watching is optional; the report arrives when the run ends whether you watched or not. The page *the run* shows one card per piece of work, titled with the promise it keeps, with a state word: *ready*, *running*, *needs you*, *passed*, *failed*, *never ran*. The header counts *N of M workers done · N of M judged on the running product*. Click a card to read its log.

image::tandem-09-run.png[The run page: cards with their state words and log counts, 5 of 10 workers done]

*needs you* means a worker stopped on a question only you can answer, about what you want, never about files or tools. The status bar says so, and the page offers a box and *Send*. xref:the-run.adoc[While it builds] says what happens in each phase and why.

== 10. Read the report

The report answers your sentences one by one, so you never read a diff to know what happened. When the run ends the strip says where it ended, such as *built, deployed and running at <address>*. The report opens with one paragraph in plain words, then *What you asked for, and what happened*: each sentence with a word, *done* in the usual case, and under it the checks that proved it.

image::tandem-13-report.png[The report of the first thing: 23 checks passed, five sentences done with the check behind each, what the work noticed, and Go on to the next on the strip]

Further down, *What the work noticed, and did not do* lists what the reviewers saw and could not settle; none of it stopped the delivery. xref:the-report.adoc[Reading the report and deciding] goes through every section and button.

== 11. Decide

The buttons at the end are yours and only yours.

* *Go on to the next* closes this thing and moves to what is left to build.
* *Take it back out* reverts this delivery's merges and pushes.
* *Run it again* starts the signed work again; nothing you wrote changes.

Press *Go on to the next*. The strip offers *Build the first* for the next thing, and the path from step 7 repeats.

== 12. When the last one is built

When the last thing is built, the strip says *N sentences · everything is built*, every sentence reads *built and accepted — it is in the project*, and the box waits for a new line.

image::tandem-14-everything-built.png[The space with everything built: three accepted things, nine sentences each built and accepted, and the strip saying so]

== What you have now

A change in your project that started as sentences, running at the app's address, with the record of what was promised, what was proved, and what you decided, kept in the space. The reviewers will have noticed things nobody asked for; xref:what-the-work-noticed.adoc[Taking up what the work noticed] is how those become the next sentences.

== Limits

* Keeping, choosing, signing and accepting are yours; Tandem's tools refuse them to Claude Code by design.
* A run is a process of its own: closing or reloading the window that started it does not end it, and *Stop* reaches it from any window.
* A page and a word on the page can differ for a while after a release; xref:words-on-the-page.adoc[The words on the page] is the reference.
