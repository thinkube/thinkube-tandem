= How a sentence becomes a promise
:page-type: concept

_What the machine does with the lines you write, shown on one of them, and why every promise it makes can be traced back to your words._

.TL;DR
Write a line such as "Finished tasks stop crowding the ones I still have to do" and press *Read these N*. The machine marks in your sentence what it is about and what must become true of it, groups sentences about the same thing, and, when you build that thing, turns each "must become true" into a promise with the files it lands in and the lines a check will prove. What you have afterwards is a promise you can read against your own sentence, and checks written before any code exists.

== The decision

A sentence a person writes has two parts, and the machine reads exactly those two: what it is about, and what must become true of it. Nothing else is read into it.

*Reading.* *Read these N* shows the sentence back with its two parts marked: the subject highlighted, the claim underlined. Under the box, *What I read in your words* lists each subject with how many claims it carries. Two sentences that name the same subject, "My tasks" and "Finished tasks", meet under it; that is how the machine knows they belong together without you saying so. A sentence that never names its subject shows the subject in brackets and the word *names nothing*, so you can see that the machine guessed. Reading records nothing; if it read you wrong, change the words and read again.

*Keeping.* *Keep these N* makes your sentences the record, word for word. Everything below is derived from them and can be derived again; the sentences themselves are never edited by the machine.

*Grouping.* *Group into things to build* proposes things worth building and judging on their own, each carrying the sentences whose subjects belong together, named in your register: *I can see at a glance what still needs doing*. Proposing decides nothing.

*Working out.* When you press *Build the first*, the machine reads the code once and, for each claim in the thing, works out a promise: one sentence saying what will be true, the files it lands in, and under it the lines that must become true. Each line says how it will be settled: *checked when it is built*, *judged by a reviewer at delivery*, or *only you can see this*. The choices your sentences left open are listed under the thing as *N things I decided for you*, each with the answer the machine took, for you to replace before you sign.

*Checks before code.* A line that reads *checked when it is built* becomes a check written by a worker that never sees the code, before the code exists. The coder builds against the promise and never reads the check. That is why a promise on the work page is the last place where saying "no, the other way round" costs nothing, and why the report can answer your sentence with *done* and the check behind it.

== Documentation is part of every thing

Every thing carries one more promise, added by the machine: *The documentation says what "<the thing>" does, in the words of the person using it.* It lands a page in your project's `docs/` folder, named after the thing, and its one line is *judged by a reviewer at delivery*: the reviewer reads the page against the running product. If nothing about the thing needs a page, press *Not needed* on that promise and say why; the press is recorded with the work. Without the page or that press, *Build these N* refuses to sign. The bottom of the work page names the page the work lands, and the report shows the documentation promise beside the others.

== What it saves you

* *No tickets and no criteria to write.* The claim in your sentence is the criterion; the machine derives the lines that prove it and shows them before anything is built.
* *No guessing at what the machine understood.* The two marks in your sentence are the whole reading; a wrong one costs a press, not a build.
* *No promise without a sentence behind it.* Every promise names the sentence it serves, and every check names the promise.
* *No undocumented change.* The page is a promise like the others, and a reviewer judges it.

== Where it shows up

* *the box*, after *Read these N*: the subjects and the marks in your sentences.
* *your sentences*: each thing with its sentences, and *N things I decided for you* under the thing in hand.
* *what it will do*: the promises, their files, their lines with how each is settled, and the documentation promise with *Not needed*.
* *the run*: a *check* card and a *code* card for each promise.
* *the report*: each sentence with *done* and its checks as chips.

== Limits

* The reading is the machine's; a sentence that says two things is read as two claims, and a sentence about nothing named shows *names nothing*.
* A thing is worked out only when you build it, so the promises of a thing you never build are never derived.
